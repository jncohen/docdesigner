Estimating Neighborhood Effects under Partial Population Interference
Joanna R. Vance1  ·  Daniel Okafor1,2  ·  Priya Menon2
1Department of Sociology, Meridian University    2Center for Population Research
July 11, 2026
Abstract. Standard estimators of neighborhood effects assume that a household's outcome depends only on its own treatment. We relax this to partial interference, allowing spillovers within but not across neighborhoods, and derive a two-stage estimator that remains consistent under moderate exposure mismatch. Applying it to a large residential-mobility experiment, we find direct effects roughly one-third smaller than the naive benchmark, and non-negligible spillover onto untreated neighbors. The gap widens with neighborhood density, suggesting that ignoring interference understates the reach of place-based policy.

1  Introduction

A large literature estimates how the neighborhood a family lives in shapes later outcomes. Almost all of it rests on an assumption borrowed from clinical trials: that one unit's treatment leaves its neighbors untouched. In residential settings this is implausible. When a household is induced to move, the families it leaves behind — and the ones it joins — plausibly experience some fraction of the same shock.

We take the interference seriously but bound it. Under partial population interference, spillovers operate within a neighborhood and stop at its boundary. This is weak enough to hold in most mobility designs and strong enough to identify both a direct effect and a spillover effect from the same data.

2  Data and methods

Our sample links administrative address histories to earnings records for 41,860 households across 612 neighborhoods. We define each household's exposure as the treated share of its neighborhood in the prior year, and estimate the following two-stage specification.

Yi = α + β·Di + γ·Ei + 𝘅i⊤δ + εi
