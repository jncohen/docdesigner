5  Discussion

Taking interference seriously changes the interpretation of a mobility experiment. The direct effect answers a narrow question — what happens to the family that moves — while the spillover speaks to the neighborhood left behind. Reporting only the first understates the policy-relevant reach of the intervention.

The gap between the two-stage and naive estimates is not an artifact of functional form. It persists under a saturated exposure specification and under trimming of the highest-density neighborhoods, where interference is most likely to bite.

Where treatment is delivered to places rather than to isolated individuals, an estimand that assumes isolation is answering the wrong question.

6  Conclusion

Partial-interference designs recover both a direct and a spillover effect from a single experiment. Ignoring the second does not merely omit a nuisance — it biases the first and hides the mechanism through which place-based policy operates.
