Threshold structure in the accumulation of household wealth

J. N. Cohen1, A. R. Delgado1,2, P. Menon2 & D. Okafor3
1Department of Sociology, Meridian University. 2Center for Population Research. 3Institute for Economic Analysis.

The relationship between education and household wealth is widely documented, yet its functional form is not. Using the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, we test whether wealth returns to schooling are continuous or concentrated at discrete credential thresholds. Net of age, race and marital status, most detectable structure is captured by two benchmarks — high-school and bachelor's-degree completion — while finer distinctions contribute little. The pattern is robust across the survey's replicate-weight design and is consistent with credentialist and institutional-sorting accounts of stratification.

Education is associated with a broad range of economic outcomes, from earnings to employment stability to intergenerational mobility1,2. That the well-educated accumulate more wealth is not in dispute; what remains unsettled is whether each additional year of schooling contributes proportionally, or whether the completion of major credentials produces discontinuities in accumulation3.

Human-capital accounts predict a continuous return: schooling supplies market-valued skills that compound over a career4. Credentialist and signalling accounts predict thresholds, in which degrees mark bearers for discretely different opportunity structures5,6.

1Results

We estimate a survey-weighted model of log net worth on credential dummies and a continuous years term. The two thresholds carry the explanatory weight; the incremental-years coefficient is small and imprecise once thresholds enter (Table 1).

The step pattern survives alternative codings of attainment and is not driven by the high-wealth oversample. Bootstrapped over the replicate weights, the ordering of threshold magnitudes is stable across resamples7.

2Discussion

That two credentials absorb most of the structure implies the wealth returns to education are not smoothly cumulative. This is consistent with sorting mechanisms in which institutional gatekeeping, rather than the marginal lesson, governs access to wealth-building positions8.

Whether the same discontinuities appear in flow measures such as saving rates, rather than the wealth stock, is a natural next test and one the SCF panel component may support9.
