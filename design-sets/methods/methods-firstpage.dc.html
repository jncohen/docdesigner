Cross-Sectional Differences in Wealth by Education

Measurement and theory alternatives
Joseph N. Cohen1
June 23, 2026
1Department of Sociology
Abstract

This note examines the empirical relationship between education and household wealth accumulation in the United States using the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances. Net of age, race, and marital status, most of the detectable structure is captured by two broad benchmarks — high-school and bachelor's-degree completion. Finer distinctions above those thresholds yield comparatively small improvements in model fit, a pattern consistent with credentialist, signaling, and institutional-sorting accounts of stratification.

Keywords  household wealth · educational attainment · Survey of Consumer Finances · measurement · stratification

1Introduction

It is well established that education and wealth move together: better-educated households are richer, and richer households acquire more education. The analytic question is the shape of that relationship. Is it best characterized as continuous — each additional year of schooling adding a proportional increment to lifetime accumulation — or as a threshold relationship, in which crossing major milestones such as high-school or college completion matters more than the marginal year?

A continuous return follows naturally from human-capital reasoning, where schooling supplies market-valued skills that compound over a career. A threshold return follows instead from accounts that emphasize credentials and institutional sorting: degrees function as markers that allocate individuals to discretely different opportunity structures. The two are observationally distinguishable, and the networth distribution in the SCF is well suited to telling them apart.
