The Wealth Gap Is a Staircase, Not a Ramp

Why two credentials — not years of schooling — explain most of the wealth divide, and what that means for policy
Joseph N. Cohen Senior Fellow · July 2026

Summary

Policy debates about education and economic mobility usually assume that more schooling means more wealth, in even measure. New analysis of the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances suggests otherwise: the wealth returns to education are concentrated at two moments — finishing high school and finishing college — and are nearly flat everywhere else.

This has direct implications for where public investment does the most good. Programs that move students across a credential threshold should yield far larger wealth effects than those that add years without a degree.

Recommendation

Target completion, not enrollment. Tie funding to credential attainment at the two thresholds where wealth effects actually appear.

What the data show

Sorting households by the education of their head produces a familiar upward gradient in net worth. But the gradient is not smooth. The jump from "some college" to a completed bachelor's degree is roughly three times the size of any single-step gradient elsewhere in the distribution — a staircase, with two tall steps, rather than a gentle ramp.

The pattern holds after adjusting for age, race, and marital status, and survives the survey's replicate-weight design. It is consistent with a large body of research on credentials and institutional sorting.
