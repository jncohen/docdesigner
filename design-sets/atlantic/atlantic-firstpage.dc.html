The American Idea

The Two Ladders

American opportunity was supposed to be a ramp. The numbers describe a staircase.
By Joseph N. Cohen

For most of a century, Americans have told a story about schooling in which effort accumulates like interest: each additional year of instruction adds a little to what a family can eventually hope to own. It is a comforting story, and a democratic one. It is also, the data suggest, wrong in an instructive way.

Sort American households by the education of the person who heads them, and net worth climbs as you would expect. But the climb is not smooth. It lurches. Between “some college” and a finished bachelor’s degree, median wealth roughly triples—a single riser far taller than any step around it. The gradient is not a ramp. It is a staircase, with two tall steps and long flat landings in between.

This matters because we fund the ramp. Public investment in higher education is overwhelmingly organized around enrollment—getting students in the door—on the theory that each year spent inside pays its own way. If the returns are gated at completion, then a year of college without a degree buys a family a place on a landing, not a step up.

“We built a ladder, and called it a ramp.”

The economists who first noticed the pattern were reluctant to make too much of it. Thresholds are the kind of thing that can appear by accident when a continuous variable is chopped into categories. But the two steps survive every reasonable way of drawing the lines—and they survive the survey’s elaborate machinery for weighting the very rich, whose fortunes might otherwise drown out everyone else.
