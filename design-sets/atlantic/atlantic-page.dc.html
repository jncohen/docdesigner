48 The Two Ladders

To see the staircase plainly, it helps to stop describing it and simply lay the numbers side by side. The jump at a completed bachelor’s degree is not subtle; it is the largest single move in the table, and it dwarfs the reward for the year of schooling that precedes it.

The Shape of the Climb

The government’s own codebook, stripped of interpretation, tells the same story in a colder voice—a set of category labels and dollar cutoffs that quietly encode where the ladder’s rungs have been placed.

EDUC   label                    net_worth_median
  3    no diploma                 38200
  4    high-school diploma       106800
  5    some college              138500
  6    bachelor's degree         312400   *threshold
  7    graduate degree           421900

What the codebook cannot say is what the pattern means for a family deciding whether a second year of community college is worth the cost. If the reward waits at the degree, then the honest answer depends entirely on whether that degree will ever arrive—and for millions of students, it never does.

A policy that funds enrollment and hopes for completion is paying full price for the landing and trusting families to find the stairs on their own.

The staircase is not a law of nature. It was built—by accreditation, by hiring conventions, by the thousand small ways an institution decides that a credential, and not the learning behind it, is the thing that counts. What was built can be rebuilt. But first we have to stop mistaking it for a ramp.
