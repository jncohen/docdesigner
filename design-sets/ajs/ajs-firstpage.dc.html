Credentials and the Architecture of Advantage1

Joseph N. Cohen

The association between education and household wealth is among the most durable regularities in social stratification, yet its functional form remains contested. Net of age, race, and marital status, most detectable structure is captured by two credential benchmarks, while finer gradations contribute little—a pattern consistent with credentialist and institutional-sorting accounts of the wealth distribution.

Introduction

Few regularities in the study of stratification are as well established as the association between education and wealth. The better educated accumulate more; the wealthy invest more heavily in the schooling of their children. What remains contested is the shape of that association—whether the wealth return to education rises smoothly with each additional year of instruction, or climbs in discrete steps as major credentials are completed.2

This distinction is not merely technical. It maps onto competing theoretical traditions with divergent implications for how opportunity is structured and where intervention might do the most good.

The Threshold Hypothesis

Human-capital theory anticipates a continuous return, as each year of schooling adds market-valued skill. Credentialist and signaling theories anticipate thresholds, as degrees sort individuals into discretely different opportunity structures.

Data and sample. The Survey of Consumer Finances is a triennial, nationally representative study of household balance sheets, with an oversample of high-wealth households and a replicate-weight design well suited to inference about the upper tail of the distribution.
