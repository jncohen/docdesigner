Cross-Sectional Differences in Wealth by Education

Measurement and theory alternatives
Joseph N. Cohen
Meridian University

Abstract  This article examines the relationship between educational attainment and household wealth in the United States using the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances. Net of age, race, and marital status, most detectable structure is captured by two credential benchmarks — high-school and bachelor's-degree completion — while finer gradations above those thresholds contribute comparatively little to model fit. The pattern is consistent with credentialist and institutional-sorting accounts of stratification.

Keywords  wealth · education · Survey of Consumer Finances · stratification

Introduction

That education and wealth move together is among the most durable findings in social stratification. The better educated are richer; the richer acquire more education. The analytic question this article takes up is not whether the association exists but what shape it takes — whether the wealth return to schooling is continuous in years, or concentrated at the crossing of major credential thresholds.

The distinction is not merely descriptive. A continuous return follows from human-capital theory, in which schooling supplies market-valued skills that accumulate with each additional year. A threshold return follows instead from credentialist and signalling accounts, in which degrees operate as markers that sort individuals into discretely different opportunity structures.

Because the two mechanisms imply observationally distinct functional forms, and because the net-worth distribution in the Survey of Consumer Finances is unusually well measured at the top, the data are well suited to adjudicating between them.

Data and Measures

The Survey of Consumer Finances is a triennial, nationally representative study of U.S. household balance sheets. Its oversample of high-wealth households, combined with a replicate-weight design, makes it the standard instrument for wealth research.

We model the natural log of net worth on indicators for high-school, bachelor's, and graduate completion, together with a continuous years-of-schooling term and controls for age, race, and marital status.
