An Essay

Credentials and the Shape of Fortune

On education, wealth, and the thresholds between them
Joseph N. Cohen

That the learned grow rich, and the rich grow learned, is a commonplace old enough to have hardened into proverb. Yet a commonplace is not an explanation, and the proverb conceals precisely the question worth asking: not whether education and wealth keep company, but in what manner they do so — whether fortune accrues to schooling by slow and even increments, or arrives instead in sudden steps, at the crossing of certain thresholds.1

The distinction is not pedantry. If each year of study adds its measured portion, then education is a kind of husbandry, and its rewards a matter of patience. But if the decisive moments are few — the diploma, the degree — then education is less a cultivation than a series of gates, and what matters is less what one learns than what one is thereby permitted to become.2

The older economic literature inclined to the first view, treating schooling as capital laid down year by year against a future return. It is an appealing picture, orderly and continuous, and it has the further merit of dignifying the whole enterprise of instruction. But appeal is not evidence, and the ledgers of actual households tell a less tidy story.

To the ledgers, then. What follows takes the plainest possible measure — the net worth of American households, sorted by the schooling of their heads — and asks of it a single question: does the curve of fortune rise as a ramp, or climb as a stair?
