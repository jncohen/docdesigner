Credentials and the Shape of Fortune

The figures, when assembled, are unambiguous in their general shape if not in every particular. Two moments in a schooling career account for the greater part of the difference in accumulated wealth: the completion of secondary school, and the taking of a first degree. Between and beyond these, the marginal year does surprisingly little.

One is tempted to moralize the result, and the temptation is worth resisting. That the degree matters more than the year does not tell us why — whether the credential certifies a genuine transformation, or merely opens a door that was always arbitrary in its guarding.3

For the man without the certificate, the door is closed however much he knows; for the man with it, open however little. The paper is not the knowledge, and yet in the economy of positions it is the paper, not the knowledge, that is weighed.

— a mid-century observer

Whether one reads this as indictment or as mere description will depend on temperament and on politics. What the numbers will not permit is the comfortable middle position — the belief that reward tracks learning smoothly, year upon year, in proportion to the labor expended.

The stair, not the ramp: it is a small finding, and an old suspicion confirmed. But confirmation has its uses, and a suspicion measured is worth more than a conviction merely held.

Meridian University
